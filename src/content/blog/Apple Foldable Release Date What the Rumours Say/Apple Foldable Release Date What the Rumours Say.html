Apple Foldable Release Date: What the Rumours Say

If you keep searching for the Apple foldable release date and getting ten different answers, that is because there still is no official one. The short version is simple: nothing is confirmed, and the most believable rumours currently point to 2026 or later. What matters is sorting the useful signals from the noise, because foldables are one of those product categories where one dramatic headline can outrun the facts in about five minutes.

Apple foldable release date: the short answer right now

Right now, there is no confirmed Apple foldable release date.

That is the answer you came for, and it is still the honest one. Apple has not announced a foldable iPhone, has not named one, and has not put anything on a public schedule. The strongest rumours cluster around 2026, with some more cautious reporting pushing the likely window into 2027.

If you want the practical takeaway, treat 2026 as the first date worth seriously watching. Treat anything earlier as optimistic unless something much more concrete shows up, such as repeated supply-chain reporting, display production timing, or multiple well-sourced forecasts lining up around the same part of the year.

Why the release date is so hard to pin down

Apple product rumours are rarely tidy, but foldable rumours are messier than usual. A normal iPhone update tends to follow a familiar rhythm: component chatter, manufacturing hints, software references, then a launch. A foldable does not fit that pattern neatly because the product itself is still a moving target.

Part of the confusion comes from how secretive Apple is with new categories. It is one thing to refresh an existing phone and quite another to introduce a device that bends in half, changes screen size as you open it, and could blur the line between an iPhone and a small tablet. That creates more prototypes, more testing stages, and more chances for half-finished ideas to leak and get mistaken for final plans.

Supply-chain leaks add another layer. A flexible display order can suggest progress, but it does not always mean a launch is around the corner. It can mean testing. It can mean Apple wants pricing estimates. It can mean suppliers are preparing capacity just in case. The same applies to hinge parts, ultra-thin glass samples, and internal components built for early engineering runs.

Then there is Apple’s broader habit of waiting. That matters here more than in almost any other category.

Apple usually waits, then arrives with a cleaner pitch

Apple is rarely obsessed with being first. You have seen that pattern before: other brands take the first swing, the market learns what works and what annoys people, then Apple turns up later with a version that tries to feel more finished.

That approach makes even more sense with foldables. Right now, foldable phones are exciting, but they still carry obvious trade-offs. Some feel too thick in your pocket. Some show a visible crease across the main screen. Some have awkward app layouts. Some cost so much that you start mentally comparing them with a phone and a tablet bought separately.

Apple tends to avoid launching into that kind of unsettled story unless it can present a cleaner one. Not just “here is a folding phone”, but “here is why this shape is useful every day, why the software fits it, and why you will keep liking it after six months”. That delay can look frustrating from the outside, but it often lines up with how Apple enters new product spaces.

Foldables are harder than a normal iPhone launch

A foldable is not just a normal phone with a hinge stuck in the middle. It is a more complicated device in almost every practical way.

Start with the hinge. That is the mechanical part that lets the phone open and close thousands of times. It has to feel smooth, hold position properly, and stay reliable over years, not just the first fortnight. Then there is crease control. The crease is the faint line or dip where the flexible screen bends. Some people stop noticing it. Some never stop noticing it. Apple is widely expected to care a lot about reducing that.

Battery layout is another headache. In a folding phone, the battery may need to be split across both halves of the device. That sounds simple enough, but it affects weight balance, internal space, heat, charging behaviour, and overall battery life. App scaling matters too. That just means apps need to look right and behave sensibly on a smaller outer screen and a larger inner one. If you open the device and an app stretches awkwardly like a cheap fitted sheet, the whole idea starts to feel less premium.

Thickness is a big deal as well. Foldables tend to be thicker than standard slab phones because you are essentially carrying two halves plus a hinge. Repairability makes things even trickier. More moving parts usually mean more things to protect, more things to align, and more expensive fixes when something goes wrong.

Put all of that together and you get the real answer to the timing mystery: Apple foldable rumours stay fuzzy because the product is genuinely hard to finish.

What the most credible rumours say about the release window

Once you strip away the wilder claims, two timeframes keep turning up: 2026 and 2027. Those are the windows that appear most often in serious coverage, and that repeat appearance matters more than any single headline.

The best way to think about current reporting is not “what exact day will it launch?” but “what release window keeps surviving repeated rumour cycles?” Right now, 2026 clearly leads that race.

The 2026 rumours

2026 is the date that keeps coming back.

That does not mean a guaranteed launch in early 2026, and honestly, that would be the surprising part. The more plausible version of the 2026 rumour is a late-2026 reveal, shipment window, or limited first release that lines up with Apple’s autumn hardware season. Reports from outlets such as CNET and MacRumors have repeatedly pointed to this broader timeframe, even when the exact details shift.

Why does 2026 keep sticking around? Because it sits in the sweet spot between “Apple has clearly explored foldables for years” and “the product still appears to need more work before launch”. It gives enough time for display suppliers to improve crease control, for hinge designs to mature, and for software work to catch up with whatever hardware Apple finally chooses.

There is also a business reason. By 2026, foldables should be less of a curiosity and more of a defined premium category. That helps Apple tell a cleaner story: not an experiment, but a deliberate high-end addition to the line-up.

The 2027 rumours

The 2027 forecasts are the cautious version of the same story. Instead of assuming Apple is almost ready, these reports assume Apple is still solving big issues, especially around the display and hinge.

That is not far-fetched. A foldable has to survive constant physical stress while still feeling elegant. If the inner screen creases too much, scratches too easily, or ages in a way Apple finds unacceptable, a delay into 2027 would make perfect sense. The same goes for manufacturing yield, which is simply the percentage of parts good enough to sell. If too many inner displays fail quality checks, launch timing slips and costs rise.

A 2027 release would also fit Apple’s habit of waiting until a category can support the kind of polished first-generation product it wants. You could call that overly cautious. You could also call it realistic.

Could it arrive earlier than that?

Could Apple surprise everyone with an earlier launch? Technically yes. Realistically, that looks unlikely.

An earlier release would suggest that development is further along than the current rumour trail implies. It would mean Apple has largely solved the hardware problems, has a strong software plan in place, and has enough supplier confidence to move toward production sooner than expected. Nothing in the most credible reporting makes that feel like the default outcome right now.

So if you see claims that the foldable is “coming very soon”, the sensible move is to look for supporting evidence. Not hype, evidence.

Which sources tend to carry the most weight

Not all rumours deserve equal attention. That sounds obvious, but in foldable coverage the difference between informed reporting and excited guessing gets blurry fast.

Some sources are closer to the supply chain. Some are better at reading Apple’s long-term strategy. Some just post dramatic claims and hope one eventually lands. If you want to judge release-date rumours properly, it helps to know what kind of source you are looking at.

Supply-chain leaks vs analyst predictions

Supply-chain leaks come from the world of parts, factories, and manufacturing planning. That includes reports about display panels, hinge components, glass suppliers, assembly capacity, and pilot production. These can be useful because physical products need physical parts, and suppliers often reveal movement before Apple ever says a word publicly.

The catch is that supply-chain information is noisy. Orders change. Testing runs happen. One supplier may prepare for a product that slips by a year. Another may get dropped entirely. So a component rumour can hint at timing, but it does not equal a launch invitation.

Analyst predictions work differently. Analysts often take multiple signals, supplier chatter, market demand, competitor timing, Apple’s broader strategy, and manufacturing trends, then turn that into a forecast. Good analysts can be genuinely useful because the job is to connect dots that casual rumour posts treat separately. But it is still a forecast, not confirmation.

Put simply, supply-chain leaks can tell you something is moving. Analyst notes can suggest what that movement might mean. Neither gives you a confirmed Apple foldable release date.

Patents are interesting, but they are not a release date

Patents attract a lot of attention because they sound official. A patent can show a folding mechanism, a display idea, a self-healing layer, or some clever hinge trick, and suddenly headlines start acting like launch day is around the corner.

That is not how patents work.

Companies patent loads of ideas. Some become products. Some become dead ends. Some exist mainly to protect an idea from competitors, even if the company never plans to ship it. A patent tells you Apple is exploring something. It does not tell you if the idea is finished, affordable, manufacturable, or attached to a shipping product.

So yes, patents are worth noticing. Just do not treat one like a calendar entry.

What Apple’s foldable is rumoured to be

The release-date question gets clearer once you ask a basic follow-up: what exactly is Apple supposed to be launching?

Most rumours point to a book-style foldable. That means a phone-sized device that opens sideways like a small book into a larger inner display, closer to a mini-tablet. A smaller clamshell flip phone has appeared in rumour cycles too, but far less consistently.

That distinction matters because the engineering and software demands differ. A book-style foldable usually aims at productivity, reading, maps, video, and multitasking. A flip phone is more about compactness and nostalgia with a modern twist.

Book-style foldable: the leading theory

The book-style design is the rumour that carries the most weight right now. Think of something that works like an iPhone when closed, then opens into a larger canvas for apps, media, browsing, notes, and split-screen tasks.

You can see why this idea appeals to Apple. It fits with existing strengths. A bigger inner display could make Safari, Messages, Maps, Photos, and video playback feel more natural. It also opens the door to stronger multitasking, something the iPhone has historically handled more conservatively than the iPad.

This format also makes more sense if Apple wants to justify a very high price. A device that acts as both premium phone and mini-tablet tells a better value story than a luxury flip phone that mainly folds for convenience.

Flip phone rumours: less common, but still around

Flip phone rumours have not completely disappeared. The idea here is a clamshell device, something more like a compact foldable that closes down small and opens into a standard phone-sized screen.

It is easy to see the appeal. A flip model could be easier to carry, more fashion-friendly, and potentially simpler to position as a lifestyle product. But current reporting leans far less heavily in this direction. If Apple is working on a foldable for an earlier first release, the book-style version looks more plausible.

That may be because the book-style model does more to expand what an iPhone can do. A flip phone mostly changes shape. A book-style foldable changes the whole experience.

What the design rumours suggest

Design rumours matter because they are not just cosmetic. In foldables, the design is directly tied to the launch timeline. Every visible detail, the crease, the hinge, the thickness, the feel in your hand, points back to the same question: is Apple satisfied yet?

The crease problem apple is reportedly trying to fix

The crease is the line or indentation where the inner screen bends. If you have handled a foldable in a shop, maybe under those bright white lights that show every fingerprint and flaw, you already know what this means. Some creases are mild. Some are easy to spot. Some are only obvious at certain angles.

Apple is widely rumoured to be especially picky here, and that sounds believable. A visible crease may be acceptable for early adopters, but Apple usually wants the first impression to feel cleaner than that. If Apple thinks the crease still looks too obvious, or feels too noticeable under your thumb, that alone could push the launch back.

It is not just about aesthetics either. The crease also reflects stress on the display material over time. If Apple wants the inner screen to age gracefully after years of opening and closing, it has to get this right.

Hinge design, thickness, and durability

A foldable hinge has to do three jobs at once. It needs to open smoothly, hold the device firmly enough during use, and protect the display when folded. That is a lot to ask from one moving part.

Rumours often suggest Apple is chasing a hinge design that reduces the crease, improves durability, and helps the phone close more neatly. That sounds technical, but the practical version is simple: you want the device to feel solid, not fiddly. You want it to sit flat enough in your pocket. You want it to keep working after thousands of open-and-close cycles without developing wobble, gaps, or grinding sounds.

Thickness is tied closely to all of this. Foldables usually end up chunkier than normal phones. Apple will almost certainly try to keep that under control, because a premium device that feels too bulky gets old fast. The trick is balancing slimness with battery size and strength. Make it too thin and battery life suffers. Make it too light and it can feel hollow. Make it too sturdy and it can turn into a brick.

Materials and overall feel

Apple’s foldable is unlikely to feel experimental in the hand, even if the technology under the surface is complex. The rumoured direction points toward premium materials, tight build quality, and a finish that fits the rest of Apple’s upper-end hardware.

That probably means a strong metal frame, carefully chosen glass on the outer surfaces, and obsessive work on weight distribution. Weight matters more here than on a normal phone because you are handling a two-part device. If one side feels heavier or the balance feels odd when open, you notice quickly.

The larger goal is obvious: Apple will want the foldable to feel like a finished luxury device, not a tech demo.

Display rumours: size, shape, and everyday use

The display is the entire point of a foldable. If the screens do not feel useful in daily life, the hinge becomes a party trick and not much else.

Most rumours point to a familiar setup for a book-style foldable: a normal-ish cover display on the outside and a larger flexible display inside. That gives you quick-use convenience when closed and a roomier workspace when open.

Outer screen and inner screen expectations

The outer screen is expected to work like your standard everyday phone display. That is where you check messages, reply quickly, use maps while walking, skip tracks, or scan through notifications without needing to unfold the device every time. If Apple gets this right, the cover display should not feel like a compromise screen. It should feel like a proper phone.

The inner screen is where the foldable earns its price. Open it up and the device could become far more comfortable for reading articles, browsing side by side, editing photos, watching video on trains, using navigation at a café table, or replying to messages while checking another app at the same time. That is the mini-tablet promise in a nutshell.

The everyday appeal is not hard to picture. Closed, it behaves like a phone. Open, it gives you more breathing room. Like carrying a notebook that doubles in size when you need to spread out.

Will it look more like an iPhone or a mini iPad?

This is one of the most interesting questions because it shapes everything else, from software to pricing to launch timing.

If Apple positions the device mainly as an iPhone that happens to unfold, you would expect a familiar interface with modest enhancements for the bigger screen. If Apple leans more toward a mini iPad experience, then multitasking, floating panels, drag-and-drop, and more flexible app layouts become much more important.

Current rumours suggest Apple may try to land somewhere in the middle. That makes sense. Too much iPad influence and the phone risks becoming awkward for quick tasks. Too much standard iPhone behaviour and the larger screen feels underused. The likely goal is a device that still feels like iOS, just with more room and smarter layout changes when you open it.

That balancing act is probably one reason the release date remains uncertain.

Software could be the real story

Hardware grabs headlines, but software will decide whether Apple’s foldable feels brilliant or gimmicky. That is the plain truth.

A folding screen is only useful if apps and system features respond intelligently when the device changes shape. Otherwise you are paying extra for more screen area and not getting much extra usefulness. Apple’s biggest opportunity may not be making the nicest hinge. It may be making the whole thing feel coherent from the first minute.

A custom iOS experience for a folding screen

Rumours have long suggested that Apple is working on software behaviour tailored to a foldable display. That probably means layouts that change smoothly when you open the device, apps that expand sensibly instead of stretching awkwardly, and interface elements that know when you are on the outer screen versus the inner one.

In plain English, you want the device to feel like it understands what just happened. If you open the phone, the app should not look surprised.

That could include rearranged toolbars, wider content panes, richer media controls, and tablet-like space for side panels in apps such as Mail, Notes, Files, or Photos. Apple is usually strong at these adaptive interface shifts, which is exactly why so many people are interested in its take on foldables in the first place.

Multitasking and app continuity

App continuity is a fancy phrase for something very simple: you start using an app on the outside screen, open the device, and the same app carries on instantly on the larger screen without losing your place. That matters more than it sounds. If every open-close action feels clumsy, the foldable loses its magic quickly.

Multitasking could be the bigger attraction. On a larger inner display, split view becomes more practical. You could have messages next to a web page, maps beside a booking email, notes beside a video, or a shopping list open while comparing prices. Drag-and-drop, if Apple leans into it, would make moving photos, links, and text between apps much faster.

For a lot of people, that is the real promise of a foldable. Not novelty, but less friction.

Why app support could delay the launch

Here is the catch: good software takes time, especially when developers outside Apple need to support it too.

Apple can make its own apps behave beautifully on day one, but a foldable also needs third-party apps to scale well. If social apps, banking apps, travel apps, and work tools all behave oddly on the larger display, your first impression sours fast. That gives Apple a strong incentive to hold the product back until developers have the tools and guidance to support it properly.

This is one of the most believable reasons for delay. Apple does not just need foldable hardware. Apple needs a foldable ecosystem that feels ready.

Price rumours: how expensive could it get?

After the release date, price is usually the next thing you want to know. And the direct answer is not especially comforting.

If Apple launches a foldable, it is expected to be expensive. Not slightly expensive. Properly premium.

The rumoured price range

Most price chatter places Apple’s foldable above the current top-end iPhone Pro models and firmly in premium foldable territory. Depending on storage and exact positioning, a starting price somewhere around £1,800 to £2,300 feels like the range most consistent with current expectations, based on the wider foldable market and repeated speculation from tech outlets including Tech Advisor and CNET.

That sounds huge because it is huge. Even by Apple standards, a foldable would likely sit in a different spending category from a normal upgrade.

Why foldables cost so much

The costs stack up quickly. Flexible displays are harder and pricier to make than standard phone screens. Hinge systems are complicated and need tight tolerances, meaning the parts have to fit together very precisely. Production volumes are also lower, especially in early generations, and lower volume usually means less room to spread manufacturing costs.

Assembly is harder too. A foldable has more moving parts, more internal design compromises, and stricter durability demands. If yield is low, meaning too many parts fail quality control, the cost per successful unit rises even further.

So if the final price feels steep, it will not be steep by accident.

Who this price would make sense for

At launch, a foldable iPhone would probably make the most sense for a fairly specific group of buyers. If you love early hardware, want Apple’s take on a new form factor, and are happy paying extra for a premium first-generation device, it could be very appealing.

The same goes if you genuinely like the idea of replacing both your phone and some iPad mini use. For travel, reading, casual work, gaming, and media, that hybrid role is easier to justify than “it folds, which is neat”.

For a basic phone upgrade, though, it is hard to imagine this being the sensible option. It looks more like a luxury choice than a mainstream one.

How it could compare with samsung and other foldables

Apple will not enter an empty market. By the time any foldable iPhone arrives, Samsung and other brands will already have years of real-world foldable experience behind them.

That matters because Apple will be judged against products you can already buy, not against a blank sheet of paper.

Samsung has the head start

Samsung has spent years refining foldables in public. That means multiple generations of hinges, displays, durability improvements, software changes, and user feedback. If you want to know what problems Apple is probably trying to solve before launch, just look at the issues foldable owners already discuss: crease visibility, battery endurance, thickness, camera compromises, app optimisation, dust resistance, and repair concerns.

Samsung’s head start gives Apple a benchmark. It also gives Apple a warning. Launch a foldable that only matches existing rivals and it will feel late. Launch one that clearly improves a few pain points and the timing suddenly looks more understandable.

Where apple could try to be different

Apple’s foldable probably will not try to win by being the first or the flashiest. The more likely strategy is to focus on areas that feel immediately noticeable in daily use.

Crease reduction is the obvious one. Battery life is another. Camera tuning matters too, because foldables sometimes sacrifice camera performance to make room for the hinge and dual-body layout. Apple could also push harder on software polish, continuity between screens, and ecosystem perks such as better handoff with Macs, AirPods, Watches, and iPads.

That is where things get interesting. A foldable iPhone does not need to outdo every rival on every spec line. It needs to feel better integrated and less compromised.

What a foldable iPhone could mean for the rest of Apple’s line-up

A foldable would not exist in isolation. Apple would have to fit it somewhere sensible inside a line-up that already includes multiple iPhones and the iPad mini.

That affects timing because Apple tends to be careful about where a new product sits, what it overlaps with, and how it avoids confusing buyers.

Would it replace any current iPhone model?

At least at first, that looks unlikely. A foldable would probably sit above the main iPhone range, not replace it.

That makes sense for both pricing and risk. Apple can introduce a foldable as a premium niche device without disrupting the standard iPhone formula that already sells in huge numbers. You can think of it less as “the new normal iPhone” and more as “the expensive alternative for people who specifically want this shape”.

Over time, if foldables become cheaper, thinner, and more durable, that could change. But early on, replacement seems much less likely than expansion.

Could it blur the line between iPhone and iPad mini?

Yes, and that is part of the appeal.

A book-style foldable could overlap with the iPad mini in obvious ways: reading books and articles, gaming, video watching, note-taking, travel use, web browsing, and quick bursts of light work. If you already like the idea of a small tablet but do not love carrying two devices, a foldable iPhone could look very tempting.

That overlap may even be intentional. Apple may not want to replace the iPad mini outright, but a foldable that handles some of those jobs could broaden the appeal of the iPhone line in an interesting way.

Reasons the launch could be delayed

Rumours often make product launches sound inevitable. Foldables are a good reminder that “in development” and “ready to sell” are very different things.

There are several practical reasons Apple could push this device back, even after years of internal work.

Manufacturing yield and screen quality

Yield is one of those factory terms that sounds dull until you realise how much it affects everything. It simply means how many units or parts come out good enough to ship.

Flexible displays are hard to make at high quality and high consistency. If too many panels show defects, fail stress tests, or fall short on crease performance, Apple has a problem. A low-yield part can delay the whole product because the company either has to wait for manufacturing to improve or accept higher costs and lower availability.

For a device expected to feel premium from day one, weak yield is not a small issue. It is a full stop.

Battery life in a thin folding body

Battery life is hard enough in a normal phone. In a thin foldable, it gets trickier.

The battery often needs to be split between two halves, while space is also being shared with a hinge, extra structural elements, cameras, and thermal management. At the same time, a larger inner display can use more power, especially if Apple wants it to be bright and smooth.

That creates a nasty balancing act. If battery life is mediocre, the device feels compromised. If the battery gets bigger, the phone can become heavier and thicker. If Apple cannot land that balance properly, delay becomes the safer option.

Long-term durability and repair concerns

Durability is one of the biggest reasons to be cautious with any foldable. A standard phone has no moving hinge, no flexible inner display exposed during use, and fewer unusual wear points. A foldable adds all of that.

Apple is likely to be especially wary of dust resistance, display wear over time, and repair complexity. If the inner screen damages too easily, or if repairs are painfully expensive, the first-generation user experience could turn sour quickly. That risk is even higher for Apple because expectations are so high to begin with.

A foldable that looks brilliant on launch day but feels fragile a year later is exactly the kind of outcome Apple usually tries to avoid.

Common misconceptions about the apple foldable release date

Foldable rumours are catnip for messy conclusions. A single leaked image or patent filing can get repeated so many times that it starts sounding like a confirmed product roadmap.

A few misconceptions keep showing up, and clearing them up makes the whole rumour picture easier to read.

“A patent means it is launching soon”

No, it does not.

A patent shows that Apple has explored an idea deeply enough to protect it legally. That could mean a future product. It could also mean an idea that never ships. Patents are evidence of experimentation, not a countdown timer.

If you see a patent headline, treat it as “Apple has looked at this” and nothing more.

“A prototype leak means the design is final”

Also no.

Prototype devices exist to test options. A company can try several hinge ideas, multiple aspect ratios, different screen layers, and various chassis shapes at once. Some prototypes are for durability. Some are for software testing. Some are just to answer one engineering question and then disappear.

So if a prototype leak shows up, it tells you development is real. It does not tell you the final design is locked.

“If rivals already sell foldables, apple must be late”

Late is not always the same as wrong.

Yes, Apple is behind rivals in time-to-market if a foldable arrives in 2026 or 2027. But Apple has often entered categories after others and focused on making the overall product story easier to understand. If that happens here, the delay will look less like hesitation and more like selection.

The better question is not “is Apple late?” It is “when Apple arrives, does the product feel complete enough to justify the wait?”

Should you wait for Apple’s foldable or buy a phone now?

This is where all the rumour talk meets a real-life decision. You want a phone, maybe soon, and the idea of a foldable iPhone sounds appealing. The problem is that rumours do not charge your phone overnight or fix a battery that dies before lunch.

So the answer depends less on excitement and more on timing, budget, and how specifically you want this exact kind of device.

Wait if you specifically want Apple’s take on foldables

If the whole point is getting a folding phone with iOS, Apple’s design approach, and tighter integration with your other Apple devices, waiting can make sense. Especially if your current phone still works well enough and you are not desperate for an upgrade.

This is even more true if you are not just chasing “new”, but specifically want a phone that opens into a larger screen for reading, travel, video, and multitasking. If that is the appeal, a standard upgrade this year may not scratch the same itch.

Buy now if your current phone is already annoying you

If your battery is fading fast, your screen is cracked, your charging port only works at one weird angle, or your phone simply feels slow every day, waiting another year or more can feel ridiculous. Keeping a failing phone while hoping for an unannounced premium foldable is a bit like hanging onto a broken umbrella through a rainy month because you saw a nicer one in a rumour post.

A normal iPhone upgrade now will almost certainly be the smarter move if your current device is already getting in the way.

A simple decision guide

Here is the easiest rule to use. Wait if you actively want a foldable and can comfortably hold off until at least 2026. Upgrade now if you mostly just need a reliable phone. Try an existing foldable from another brand if you want the form factor sooner and are open to leaving iOS.

That rule will get you closer to a good decision than any dramatic leak.

The bottom line on apple foldable release date rumours

The cleanest takeaway is this: 2026 looks like the Apple foldable release date window worth watching, but nothing is official until Apple says so. A shift into 2027 would not be shocking, especially if Apple is still refining the hinge, the crease, battery life, or software support.

If you are deciding what to do with your next upgrade, ignore the hype for a minute and look at your current phone honestly. If it still feels fine, waiting another cycle could be worth it. If it is already making daily life irritating, buy the phone that solves your problem now and treat the foldable as a future bonus, not a plan you have to build your year around.
